Karnataka HC Upholds Property Attachment Over Unpaid K-RERA Dues

By the Luxura Habitat Team•November 1, 2026•7 Min Read

Winning a case before the Karnataka Real Estate Regulatory Authority is one thing. Actually collecting the money a developer has been ordered to pay is often a separate, longer fight. A recent Karnataka High Court ruling is a useful reminder of how that second fight is supposed to work, and that the courts are willing to back it. In Writ Petition No. 6366 of 2025, decided on October 6, 2026, the High Court dismissed a challenge by the developer Unishire Promoters against the attachment of its property, ordered after it failed to pay a homebuyer the amount due under a final K-RERA order, according to reporting from LiveLawBiz.

1. How a RERA Order Actually Gets Enforced

A RERA order isn't a civil court decree, and it isn't self-executing. If a developer simply doesn't pay what K-RERA has ordered, the buyer's recourse runs through a specific revenue-recovery mechanism: applying for a recovery certificate under Section 40 of the RERA Act, read with Rule 25 of the Karnataka RERA Rules. Once that certificate is issued, the Deputy Commissioner is obligated to recover the amount as if it were an arrear of land revenue, a route that can escalate to directing the local Tahsildar to attach the developer's property. It's the same underlying mechanism the Karnataka High Court reaffirmed separately in April 2026, in Rajesh Rao C.V. v. State of Karnataka, where it held that once a recovery certificate is issued, the concerned authorities are obligated to take appropriate steps to recover the amount, rather than treating the certificate as optional or discretionary.

2. What Happened in the Unishire Case

The timeline laid out in press coverage of the ruling shows how this mechanism played out in practice:

  • A K-RERA order went unpaid. The developer, Unishire Promoters, did not pay dues it owed a homebuyer under a K-RERA order.
  • The Deputy Commissioner ordered recovery. On April 10, 2024, the Deputy Commissioner passed a recovery order treating the dues as revenue arrears.
  • The Tahsildar attached the property. The Yelahanka Tahsildar issued an attachment order against the developer's property on January 16, 2025.
  • The developer's appeal against the underlying order failed. On August 23, 2025, the developer's appeal against the original K-RERA order was dismissed, leaving that order final.
  • The developer then challenged the attachment itself. Rather than accept the attachment, the developer took the matter to the Karnataka High Court, arguing the Tahsildar's seizure lacked legal grounds.
  • The High Court dismissed that challenge on October 6, 2026. It found the developer had produced no material showing the underlying K-RERA order had been successfully set aside, and that a final, unchallenged order could not be reopened indirectly by attacking the attachment that flowed from it.

3. What the Court Left Open

The ruling wasn't an absolute door-slam on the developer. The court noted that it could still challenge the underlying K-RERA order concerning the property, if otherwise entitled to under law, through the correct legal route. What the judgment makes clear is that a developer cannot use a challenge to the enforcement step, the attachment, as a substitute for actually overturning the order that triggered it. Once an order has attained finality because an appeal against it has been dismissed, the time to contest its merits has effectively passed.

4. Why This Matters If You're Chasing an Unpaid RERA Award

For homebuyers who have already secured a K-RERA order, whether for a refund, interest on a delayed possession, or compensation, and are now dealing with a developer who simply isn't paying, this ruling is a concrete signal that the revenue-recovery route works and that the courts will uphold it against a developer's attempt to re-litigate a settled order through the back door. The practical next step after a K-RERA order attains finality isn't to wait and hope the developer complies voluntarily; it's to apply for a recovery certificate and push the process through the Deputy Commissioner's office, which is exactly the mechanism this ruling has now reinforced.

The Bottom Line

A K-RERA order is only as useful as a homebuyer's ability to enforce it, and the revenue-recovery mechanism, recovery certificate to Deputy Commissioner to Tahsildar attachment, is the tool the law actually provides for that. The Karnataka High Court has now backed that mechanism against a direct legal challenge from a developer, confirming that once a RERA order is final, the attachment that follows from non-payment is not something a developer can unwind by contesting the attachment itself.
