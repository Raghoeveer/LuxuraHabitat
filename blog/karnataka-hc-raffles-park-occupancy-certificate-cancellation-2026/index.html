Raffles Park OC Cancellation: Why Buyers Must Verify Occupancy Certificates

By the Luxura Habitat Team•November 1, 2026•7 Min Read

An occupancy certificate is meant to be the final word on whether a building is actually fit to live in. A recent Karnataka High Court ruling shows just how unsettled that "final" word can remain for years. The court has upheld the cancellation of occupancy certificates (OCs) for 33 villas at the Raffles Park project in Hoskote taluk, on Bengaluru's eastern periphery, and in doing so recalled its own earlier order that had restored those same certificates, according to reporting from LiveLawBiz. The certificates in question were first issued back in 2016 and 2017. This dispute has now run for roughly a decade, which is exactly the kind of timeline that should make any buyer pause before treating an OC as settled simply because it exists on paper.

1. How This Case Unfolded

  • 2016-2017: The Samethanahalli Gram Panchayat issued occupancy certificates for 33 of the villas at Raffles Park.
  • Those OCs were later cancelled by the authorities, prompting the developer, Raffles Residency, to challenge the cancellation in court.
  • December 11, 2023: The Karnataka High Court quashed the cancellation and restored the OCs, but with conditions: 15 villas were to be made fit for occupation within three months, and the remaining 18 within a further three months.
  • Latest ruling: The High Court has now upheld the cancellation of the same 33 OCs and recalled its own 2023 order, finding that the architect's completion certificate relied on earlier did not actually reflect the project's real condition, and that no fresh notice to the developer was required before the cancellation could stand.

2. It Wasn't a Uniform Outcome Across the Project

Two villa buyers were permitted to join the case as parties, and the court specifically carved out Villas 8 and 53 as not covered by the cancellation. That detail matters beyond this particular project: it shows that an OC dispute at a development doesn't necessarily apply identically to every unit within it. A buyer evaluating resale or an under-construction unit at a project with any history of OC litigation needs to check the status of the specific unit, not just the project's reputation or its original launch-era paperwork. The court also left open, for the Real Estate Regulatory Authority to decide separately, whether the project qualifies as an "ongoing project" under RERA, a question with its own set of consequences for buyer protections.

3. The Due-Diligence Lesson for Anyone Buying in Bengaluru

The specific failure the court identified is instructive: an architect's completion certificate, the document developers often rely on to certify that construction matches approved plans, did not actually reflect the real condition of these villas. That's a reminder that paperwork asserting a building is complete and fit for occupation isn't automatically reliable just because it exists and was accepted once. For any buyer, especially for villa and plotted developments on the city's outer edges where panchayat-level approvals are more common than in core BBMP/GBA wards, it's worth treating the occupancy certificate as something to independently verify with the issuing authority rather than take on the developer's or broker's word.

  • Ask for the actual OC, not just a possession letter. A builder offering possession is not the same as a building holding a valid, uncontested occupancy certificate.
  • Check for pending litigation. An OC that has been challenged, cancelled, or restored at any point in a project's history is worth flagging and investigating before you commit, even if the current status looks resolved.
  • Verify unit by unit where relevant. As this case shows, a cancellation or restoration doesn't automatically apply uniformly to every unit in a project.

The Bottom Line

Nine or ten years after a set of villas first received their occupancy certificates, their legal status is still being argued in the Karnataka High Court. That's an unusually long runway for what should be a settled, fairly mechanical approval, and it's a useful reminder that OC status deserves the same scrutiny buyers typically reserve for RERA registration and title documents. An occupancy certificate (OC) is issued by the local civic or planning authority confirming a building was constructed in line with approved plans and is fit for people to live in. Without a valid OC, a unit is not legally fit for occupation even if the developer has physically handed over possession, which can affect everything from utility connections to resale and the unit's standing under RERA.
