RERA at 10 Years: What's Actually Changed for Bengaluru and Chennai Buyers

By the Luxura Habitat Team•November 23, 2026•8 Min Read

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act came into force on May 1, 2016, which put its tenth anniversary in May 2026. Commentary marking that milestone has continued through the second half of the year, including a Drishti IAS analysis published November 18, 2026, and it paints a genuinely mixed picture. On the positive side, cumulative project registrations nationwide have crossed 1.6 lakh, up from roughly 1.4 lakh in January 2025, and various estimates put resolved consumer complaints somewhere between 1 lakh and 1.5 lakh since the Act's inception. On the less flattering side, the Forum for People's Collective Efforts, a prominent homebuyer advocacy body, used the anniversary to report that more than 75% of State RERA Authorities have either never published annual reports, discontinued doing so, or let them go stale, and has written to the Prime Minister seeking a split in the regulator's mandate between counting registrations and disclosures on one hand, and actively enforcing against violating builders on the other.

1. The Headline Numbers Are Real, But They Measure Activity, Not Outcomes

A rise from 1.4 lakh to 1.6 lakh registered projects in a little over a year is a genuine sign that RERA registration has become a normalised, expected part of launching a project in most of India, including Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, the two states this site covers most closely. But a registration count measures compliance with a disclosure requirement, not whether a project is actually being delivered on time or whether a buyer who files a complaint gets a timely resolution. FPCE's core argument, that over three-quarters of state authorities have let their own annual reporting lapse, cuts directly against treating the registration count alone as proof that the system is working well everywhere.

2. Karnataka and Tamil Nadu Are Comparatively Active, Not Comparatively Perfect

Within this uneven national picture, Karnataka's K-RERA and Tamil Nadu's TNRERA are both counted among the more functional state authorities, routinely registering new projects and processing buyer complaints, unlike several north-eastern states and Ladakh, which still lacked a permanent RERA authority as of mid-2026. This site has covered specific K-RERA and TNRERA developments as they happen this year, from Karnataka's own quarterly disclosure deadlines to Tamil Nadu's advertising-penalty rules and its new permanent RERA chairperson, and the pattern across those stories is a regulator that is active but still working through its own process maturity, rather than one that has nothing left to fix.

  • Registrations: 1.6 lakh+ nationally by May 2026, up from ~1.4 lakh in January 2025.
  • FPCE finding: 75%+ of State RERA Authorities have never published, discontinued, or failed to update annual reports.
  • Gaps: Several north-eastern states and Ladakh still lacked a permanent RERA authority as of mid-2026.
  • Karnataka and Tamil Nadu: Both comparatively active, but buyers should still verify project-level status directly rather than assume uniform enforcement.

3. What a Decade of RERA Has Actually Fixed

It would be wrong to read the FPCE critique as RERA having failed outright. Before 2016, a buyer had essentially no standardised way to verify a project's land title, approvals, or escrow arrangement for construction funds beyond trusting the developer's own brochure. Today, in both Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, a buyer can look up a project's RERA number, see its disclosed timeline, and in principle file a complaint through a defined tribunal process rather than relying solely on civil court litigation, which could take years longer. That structural shift, from zero standardised disclosure to a searchable public registry with a dedicated grievance mechanism, is real progress, even if the mechanism's actual enforcement speed and consistency still vary considerably by state and by how persistently a buyer pursues a complaint.

4. What This Means for You, Practically, Today

The decade-in-review commentary is a reminder to keep doing the basic diligence RERA was designed to enable, rather than assuming registration alone settles the matter. Before booking in either city, look up the project's RERA number directly on the Karnataka RERA portal (rera.karnataka.gov.in) or the Tamil Nadu RERA portal (tnrera.in), not just on the developer's own website, and check the quarterly disclosure filings for any flagged construction delays. If a dispute does arise, know that both state authorities have a functioning complaint process, even if FPCE's findings suggest the reporting and oversight layer above individual states still has real gaps a decade in.

The Bottom Line

Ten years on, RERA has fundamentally changed what a buyer can verify before booking in Bengaluru or Chennai, replacing brochure-only trust with a searchable public registry and a dedicated complaint process. It has not, per FPCE's own late-2026 findings, achieved consistent oversight across every state, and registration numbers alone should not be mistaken for a guarantee of enforcement quality. The practical response for any individual buyer is unchanged by this debate: verify the RERA number yourself, read the disclosure filings, and don't treat "RERA registered" as the end of your diligence rather than its starting point.
