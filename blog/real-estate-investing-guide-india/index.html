Real Estate Investing in India: A Practical Guide for South India Buyers

By the Luxura Habitat Team•September 19, 2026•11 Min Read

Searching "real estate investing" turns up a flood of generic advice built around US markets, REITs and mortgage products that don't map cleanly onto how property actually gets bought, financed and taxed in India. If you're evaluating a plot, apartment or villa in Bangalore, Chennai or Coimbatore, the mechanics are different enough that the generic advice can lead you astray. This guide covers what actually matters here: how returns really break down, what RERA does and doesn't protect you from, how financing differs by asset type, what changes if you're an NRI, and which corridors are worth watching heading into 2026.

1. The Four Ways to Invest in Real Estate in India

Before comparing locations, it helps to be clear on the asset type, since each carries a different risk, liquidity and return profile.

  • Residential plots and land. Buying a plot in an approved, RERA-registered layout to hold or self-build. Highest long-term appreciation potential and lowest holding cost, but no rental income until built, and financing covers a smaller share of the price.
  • Apartments. Ready or under-construction flats. Easier to finance, easier to rent out, and easier to exit, but appreciation is generally steadier and slower than plotted land, and monthly maintenance charges are a permanent cost.
  • Villas and independent houses. A middle ground: you own the land under a built structure, so you get land-value appreciation plus a livable asset, at a materially higher entry price than an equivalent plot or apartment.
  • REITs (Real Estate Investment Trusts). Listed vehicles that let you invest in commercial real estate through the stock market in small denominations, with no physical property to manage. Worth knowing about for portfolio diversification, though they expose you to office and retail markets rather than the residential plots, apartments and villas most South India buyers are actually comparing.

Most first-time investors in Bangalore and Chennai are choosing between the first three. The right one depends less on which is "best" in the abstract and more on your holding period and whether you need rental income along the way.

2. Rental Yield vs. Capital Appreciation: How to Actually Measure Returns

The single most common mistake in real estate investing is judging a property on price appreciation alone, or on rental yield alone, without weighing both together against your actual holding period.

The India-Specific Reality

Residential rental yields across most Indian metros, Bangalore and Chennai included, typically run in the 2% to 4% range annually. That's low by global standards, which is why most of the total return in Indian residential property has historically come from capital appreciation, not rent. If you're underwriting a purchase purely on rental yield the way you might a US rental property, the numbers here will disappoint you.

That doesn't make rental income irrelevant, it's what services part of your loan EMI and cash-flows the holding period, but it does mean the investment case for a specific plot, apartment or villa has to rest primarily on a credible appreciation thesis: genuine infrastructure or employment drivers in that specific corridor, not just a developer's brochure projection.

3. RERA: The First Filter for Any Investment

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act requires most residential projects above a minimum size to register with the relevant state RERA authority, Karnataka's K-RERA or Tamil Nadu's TNRERA depending on where you're buying, before advertising or accepting bookings. Registration means the developer has disclosed the land title, the sanctioned layout plan, the promised amenities and a legally binding possession date, and is required to deposit a defined share of buyer collections into an escrow account earmarked specifically for that project's construction.

Before you invest in anything, check the project's RERA registration number directly on the state RERA portal, not just on the developer's own website, and confirm the promised possession date matches what you're being told verbally. A project still listed as "proposed" or without a RERA number carries meaningfully higher risk of delay, litigation or fund diversion, regardless of how established the developer's brand is.

4. Financing: What Changes Between a Plot, an Apartment and a Villa

Banks treat land and constructed property differently, and it materially changes how much cash you need upfront.

Asset TypeTypical Loan-to-ValueHome Loan Interest DeductionRental Income Potential
Plot / Land~60-70%Not available until construction is completeNone until built
Apartment~80-90%Available (Section 24, subject to limits for self-occupied property)Immediate on possession
Villa / Independent House~75-85%Available once construction is completeImmediate on possession

Because land loans cover a smaller share of the purchase price and don't carry the same interest deduction until you build, plot buyers should budget for a materially larger cash contribution as a share of the purchase price than an apartment buyer making the same rupee investment. This is one of the most common gaps in first-time investors' math: modelling a plot purchase using an apartment's financing assumptions.

5. Real Estate Investing for NRIs

A meaningful share of demand in Bangalore and Chennai's premium micro-markets comes from NRI buyers, and the rules differ in a few specific ways worth knowing before you commit:

  • What you can buy. Under FEMA, NRIs and OCIs can purchase residential and commercial property freely, but not agricultural land, farmhouses or plantation property, without separate RBI approval.
  • How you pay. Purchases are typically routed through an NRE, NRO or FCNR bank account, and payment in foreign currency directly, or cash, is not permitted.
  • Tax on exit. TDS on sale proceeds is generally higher for NRI sellers than for resident sellers, and is deducted at the point of sale regardless of your actual capital gain, with any excess reclaimable through a tax return.
  • Repatriating proceeds. Sending sale proceeds back abroad is allowed but subject to RBI's remittance limits and documentation, including a chartered accountant's certification, so plan the exit process before you buy, not after.

None of this makes NRI investing meaningfully harder, but it does mean the paperwork and tax withholding are different from what a resident Indian buyer experiences, and it's worth confirming with your bank and CA upfront rather than discovering the details at the time of sale.

7. Due Diligence Checklist Before You Invest

Whichever corridor and asset type you land on, run through this before booking:

  • RERA registration. Verify the number directly on the state RERA portal, not the developer's brochure.
  • Title and approvals. Confirm clear title, a sanctioned layout plan, and (for apartments) an approved building plan and occupancy certificate status.
  • Encumbrance Certificate (EC). Check for any pending loans, litigation or disputes registered against the property.
  • Khata / land conversion status. For plots, confirm the land has been converted from agricultural to non-agricultural use and carries a proper khata.
  • Developer track record. Look at whether the same developer's previous projects delivered on the promised timeline, not just their brand reputation.
  • Actual, not promised, infrastructure. Distinguish between roads, metro lines or industrial investments that are already operational versus ones still at the planning or tendering stage.

8. Common Mistakes First-Time Real Estate Investors Make

  • Underwriting on rental yield alone. Given typical 2-4% yields in Indian metros, a purely yield-driven case rarely stacks up against fixed-income alternatives; the real case has to rest on appreciation.
  • Applying apartment financing assumptions to a plot purchase. Land's lower loan-to-value catches buyers who haven't budgeted for a larger cash outlay.
  • Trusting a single headline appreciation statistic. Property portal figures for the same corridor often vary meaningfully by source; use them directionally, not as a precise number to underwrite against.
  • Skipping RERA verification because the developer is well-known. Brand size reduces but doesn't eliminate execution risk on any individual project.
  • Ignoring exit liquidity. Earlier-stage corridors typically have fewer active buyers if you need to sell within a short window; factor that into your holding-period plan before you buy, not after.

What This Means for You

Real estate investing in South India rewards buyers who match the asset type and corridor stage to their own holding period, financing capacity and risk tolerance, and who verify RERA status, title and developer track record before committing rather than after. There's no single "best" answer between a plot in an earlier-stage corridor and an apartment in a mature one, both can work, depending on what you're actually optimizing for.
