RBI's December MPC Meeting: Why a Rate Hike, Not a Cut, Is Now on the Table

By the Luxura Habitat Team•November 11, 2026•8 Min Read

The Reserve Bank of India's Monetary Policy Committee is scheduled to meet December 2 to 4, 2026, confirmed in the RBI's own October policy minutes, and for the first time in a long stretch, the conversation ahead of a meeting isn't about how much further rates might fall. SBI Research has flagged a possible 50 basis point tightening move, delivered either as a single 50 bps repo hike or as a 25 bps hike combined with a 25 bps widening of the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) corridor, while Bank of Baroda has gone further, forecasting a rate-hike cycle beginning in December with cumulative tightening of 50 to 75 basis points. For home loan borrowers in Bangalore and Chennai who have spent the better part of the last year watching EMIs fall or hold steady, this is a genuine change in the direction of the conversation worth understanding before the meeting, not after it.

1. Why the Narrative Has Flipped From Cuts to Hikes

Through much of 2025 and into 2026, the RBI's rate-setting conversation was dominated by cuts: a December 2025 reduction brought the repo rate down to 5.25%, and lenders including HDFC and SBI passed on lower rates to floating-rate borrowers through the first half of 2026. That easing cycle appears to be running into a different set of pressures now. SBI Research's 50 bps hike call and Bank of Baroda's forecast of a December-starting tightening cycle both point to inflation concerns outweighing the growth-support case that justified the earlier cuts. A Reuters poll cited by one market-data tracker reportedly found 29 of the 53 economists surveyed expecting at least one further 25 bps increase at the December meeting; we'd flag that we sourced this specific figure from a secondary tracking site rather than the original poll, so treat the exact number as indicative of a shift in sentiment rather than a settled consensus.

2. What's Actually Uncertain Right Now

Here's where we want to be candid about the limits of what we could independently confirm: our own research turned up conflicting reports on where the repo rate currently stands. One source describes a 25 bps hike to 5.50% effective October 7, 2026, while others describe the rate as having been held at 5.25% through the year without a clearly dated change since the December 2025 cut. We were not able to reconcile these two accounts from publicly available reporting, which is itself a useful reminder that even professional market trackers can disagree on a figure as basic as the current policy rate during a period of genuine uncertainty. Borrowers and buyers should treat any single secondary report, including this one, as a starting point, and confirm the current repo rate directly from the RBI's own announcements before making a financing decision.

  • The direction of the conversation has changed, even if the exact number hasn't been confirmed. Multiple independent forecasters are now discussing hikes, not cuts, which is itself the more important signal for planning purposes.
  • A 50-75 bps cumulative tightening, if it materialises, would be meaningful. That is a larger move than the RBI typically makes in a single cycle, and would reverse a good portion of the relief floating-rate borrowers have seen since late 2025.
  • Confirm your own loan's current rate and reset terms with your lender. Not every floating-rate loan resets on the same schedule, so a December rate change may not hit your EMI immediately.

3. What a Hike Would Mean for Home Loan EMIs in Bangalore and Chennai

The arithmetic of a rate hike is straightforward even if the policy debate isn't. On a Rs 75 lakh home loan over a 20-year tenure, each 25 basis point increase in the applicable rate typically adds somewhere in the range of Rs 1,200 to Rs 1,400 to the monthly EMI, depending on the loan's current rate and remaining tenure; a full 50 bps move would roughly double that impact, and a 75 bps move would be larger still. For a mid-segment Bangalore apartment in the Rs 80 lakh to Rs 1.5 crore range, or a comparable Chennai OMR-corridor home, that is a real enough number to factor into a monthly budget, though it is modest relative to the total cost of the property itself. Buyers currently comparing a project like Sattva Aeropolis in Bangalore's airport corridor against a similarly positioned Chennai OMR project should ask their lender directly how a December rate change, if it happens, would apply to their specific sanction, since reset timing and benchmark choice (repo-linked versus MCLR-linked) vary by bank and by when the loan was originally sanctioned.

It's also worth keeping this in perspective: even in a 50-75 bps hike scenario, India's lending rates would remain within a range most borrowers have navigated before over the last decade, and a rate move doesn't change a specific project's own RERA status, price sheet or construction timeline. The more useful response for most buyers is a practical one: get a clear, current EMI quote from your lender before committing to a purchase timeline, rather than guessing at what December might bring.

The Bottom Line

After a year in which the RBI's rate story was almost entirely about relief for home loan borrowers, the run-up to the December 2-4, 2026 MPC meeting has flipped the script, with serious forecasters now debating a hike rather than a cut. The exact size and even the current starting point remain genuinely unsettled in public reporting, so the right response isn't panic, it's precision: confirm your loan's current terms, ask your lender how a rate change would flow through, and keep that number in context against the much larger decision of which project and city actually fit your needs. Most floating-rate home loans in India are linked to an external benchmark tied to the repo rate, so a repo rate increase typically feeds through to a higher loan interest rate within a lending cycle, usually a quarter. Depending on your bank's reset policy, this shows up either as a higher EMI on the same tenure or an extended tenure at the same EMI. On a Rs 75 lakh, 20-year loan, each 25 basis point increase works out to roughly Rs 1,200 to Rs 1,400 more per month, depending on the loan's remaining tenure and current rate.

A looming rate decision is a reasonable prompt to get your financing in order, but it shouldn't be the only reason to rush a property decision. If you're close to finalising a purchase and comfortable with the project's RERA status, price sheet and builder track record, locking your loan sanction before any potential hike takes effect can make sense. If you're still evaluating the property itself, it's generally not worth compromising on due diligence just to beat a rate announcement that, as of now, remains a forecast rather than a confirmed outcome.

A floating-rate loan moves with the lender's external benchmark, which in turn tracks the RBI's repo rate, so your EMI or tenure can rise or fall over the life of the loan. A fixed-rate loan locks in an interest rate for a specified period, insulating you from near-term RBI moves but typically starting at a higher rate than a comparable floating option and usually reverting to floating after the fixed period ends. With rate-hike chatter building ahead of December's MPC meeting, some borrowers may want to ask their lender for fixed-rate quotes to compare, though the right choice still depends on your personal risk tolerance and loan tenure.
