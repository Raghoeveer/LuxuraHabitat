NRI Property TDS: PAN Replaces TAN From October 1, 2026

By the Luxura Habitat Team•October 29, 2026•7 Min Read

A compliance change that quietly took effect on October 1, 2026 is worth knowing about if you're buying property from a Non-Resident Indian seller in Bangalore or Chennai. Under the new Income-tax Act, 2025, a resident individual or HUF buying property from an NRI can now deduct and deposit the required TDS using their own PAN, through a challan-cum-statement process similar to the familiar Form 26QB, rather than first having to apply for a Tax Deduction Account Number (TAN). Multiple tax advisory firms, including Vakilsearch and specialist NRI tax consultants, have written about the change, tying it to Section 397(1)(c) of the new Act. We could not independently verify the underlying gazette notification, so if you're relying on this for an active transaction, confirm the specifics with a chartered accountant rather than this piece alone.

1. Why the TAN Requirement Was a Real Friction Point

Until September 2026, a resident buyer purchasing from a resident seller used the simple, PAN-based Form 26QB process to deduct 1% TDS under Section 194-IA. But the moment the seller was an NRI, the transaction fell under Section 195 instead, and the buyer was generally required to obtain a TAN first, a separate registration that could take days to weeks to process and added an extra administrative step most first-time resident buyers had never dealt with before. For deals under time pressure, especially where an NRI seller wanted to close quickly before returning abroad, that TAN application was a common source of delay.

2. What Actually Changes From October 1

  • No separate TAN application. The buyer can now deduct and deposit TDS on the purchase using their existing PAN, through a mechanism tax advisories describe as similar to Form 26QB.
  • The TDS rate itself is unchanged. This is a compliance simplification, not a tax cut. The rate that applies to a sale from an NRI seller under Section 195 stays the same; only the paperwork for depositing it is simpler.
  • A no-PAN seller still triggers a higher deduction. Tax advisory commentary on the new Act notes that if the NRI seller doesn't have a valid PAN, the buyer must deduct at the higher of the applicable rate or 20%, under Section 397(2)(b)(i), the same logic that already exists for the resident-seller TDS regime.
  • Deals before October 1 follow the old route. If your sale deed was executed before the cut-off, the earlier TAN-based Section 195 process applies, not this one.

3. Why This Matters for Bangalore and Chennai Buyers Specifically

Both cities have a meaningful base of NRI sellers and buyers, whether it's an NRI parent exiting an inherited flat in Chennai or an NRI family divesting from a Bangalore apartment ahead of a move back to India. Any friction in the TDS process on these transactions tends to show up as last-minute registration delays, which is exactly the kind of practical, unglamorous problem this change is aimed at. A simpler PAN-based route should, in principle, make it easier for a resident buyer to close on time when the seller happens to be an NRI, without changing how much tax is actually withheld.

The Bottom Line

This is a compliance fix, not a tax policy shift: the amount of TDS due on a property purchase from an NRI seller hasn't changed, only the mechanics of depositing it have gotten simpler from October 1, 2026. If you're in the middle of, or about to start, a purchase from an NRI seller in Bangalore or Chennai, it's worth raising this directly with your chartered accountant or the registering sub-registrar's office so the correct process is used for your closing date.
