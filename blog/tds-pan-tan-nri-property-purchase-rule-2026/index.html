Buying Property From an NRI Seller Gets Simpler From October 1, 2026

By the Luxura Habitat Team•September 21, 2026•6 Min Read

Starting October 1, 2026, resident individuals and Hindu Undivided Families buying immovable property from a non-resident seller will be able to deduct and deposit the applicable tax deducted at source using their existing PAN, instead of first having to apply for a separate Tax Deduction and Collection Account Number, or TAN. The change falls under the Income Tax Act, 2025, which replaced the 1961 Act from April 1, 2026, and has been reported by ClearTax, Integrated India and other tax advisory outlets. It's a compliance simplification rather than a tax change, but for anyone in the process of, or considering, a purchase from an NRI seller, it's worth understanding exactly what shifts and what doesn't.

1. What Was Required Before

Under the earlier framework, a buyer responsible for deducting TDS on certain transactions, including property purchases from a non-resident seller, typically needed a TAN to deposit that tax with the government. For an individual buyer completing what is often a one-time property purchase, that meant a separate registration process layered on top of the transaction itself, adding paperwork and time to what should otherwise be a straightforward closing.

2. What Changes From October 1, 2026

From October 1, 2026, eligible resident buyers, individuals and HUFs, can use their own PAN to deduct and deposit TDS on a property purchase from a non-resident seller, without first obtaining a TAN. This doesn't change how much tax is owed. It changes which identifier the buyer uses to remit that tax, removing an administrative step that previously added friction to transactions that are already more procedurally involved than a standard resident-to-resident sale.

3. A Related but Separate Change: The New Form 141

It's worth distinguishing this from another related update that took effect earlier in the year. From April 1, 2026, the older Form 26QB used for TDS on property transactions was replaced by a new Form 141, which applies specifically to property deals worth Rs 50 lakh or more and allows a buyer to file a single form per transaction even when there are multiple sellers involved. That change is separate from the October 1 PAN-instead-of-TAN update, though both are part of the same broader push to simplify property-transaction tax compliance under the new Income Tax Act.

  • No change to how much tax is owed. TDS rates applicable to non-resident sellers are unaffected by this update, only the compliance mechanism for depositing that tax has changed.
  • One less registration step for a one-time buyer. If you're purchasing a single property from an NRI seller and had no other reason to hold a TAN, this removes a process that previously added time to closing.
  • Still confirm specifics with a professional. TDS obligations on NRI seller transactions involve rate slabs, lower-deduction certificates and other specifics that vary by transaction. Always verify your exact obligations with a chartered accountant before completing the purchase.

Why This Matters for South India's Buyer Pool

NRI participation in India's residential market has been a consistent theme through 2026, and a meaningful share of resale and even some primary transactions in Bengaluru and Chennai involve an NRI seller on the other side of the table. Anything that reduces friction in that specific transaction type is a practical, if modest, win for resident buyers navigating what can already be a more document-heavy process than a typical resident-to-resident purchase.

The Bottom Line

This is a genuine, dated compliance change with a clear effective date of October 1, 2026, and it's worth knowing about if an NRI seller is involved in a purchase you're currently working through. It simplifies paperwork rather than changing tax liability, so plan your transaction timeline accordingly and always confirm exact obligations with a tax professional.
